1. The idea in one minute

A to-do list says what to do. The Goals Board says what will be true, and by when:

עד יום שבת בערב הארגז יהיה במחסן · עד מחר בבוקר יש לי שוקולד חדש במקרר

Each goal gets a picture of the finished state, a countdown, and the next small step. Picturing the result alone can lower your energy, so the board always pairs it with the way there: the steps, the likely obstacle, and a plan for it [2, 3, 4, 5].

2. Start: your first goal

  1. Tap the flag in the top bar.
  2. Type or say one sentence: "By tomorrow morning there is new chocolate in the fridge."
  3. The board makes a card: a drawing, the deadline (tomorrow 08:00), and asks for one first step with a time: "Buy it at the supermarket on the way home · today 18:30."
  4. When you begin, tap Started. When it is true, tap Done and, if you like, take an "after" photo.

Pictures. The drawing is built from an object and a place found in your sentence (box + storage room, chocolate + fridge). You can replace any drawing with your own photo. AI-generated images may come later; they would send your sentence to an outside service and cost money per image.

How many at once. We suggest about three active goals while you are learning the board. It is a suggestion, not a lock.

A done-state is something you could photograph or check. "The box is in the storage room" passes. "Be more organised" does not.

4. Going deeper, when you want

The board works like an automatic gearbox, except you choose the gear for each goal. A simple goal stays one card and one step. A bigger one can be taken apart backward from the finish, which helps most with complex goals [6]. There are no unlock conditions: add as much structure as the goal needs.

Plan backward

Tap Plan backward and answer one question again and again: "What must be true just before this?" Stop when the answer is something you can do now. The dates spread back from the deadline; you can move them.

One obstacle, one plan

For each goal, name the most likely obstacle and write an if–then plan: "If I'm tired on Saturday, then I only load the car and drive." This pairing (wish, outcome, obstacle, plan) is the part of the research with the steadiest effect [1, 4]. Some obstacles turn into steps ("the key is at my brother's" becomes "key in my bag, Friday").

The one-minute rehearsal (optional)

Open a card full-screen: look at the finished picture for a moment, then walk the path from now forward, read your if–then plan, and tap Started. Rehearsing the process works better than only imagining the result [5].

How progress is shown

Early on the card shows what is done ("1 of 4 done"); near the end it shows what is left ("one step left"). People push harder near a goal, and which part you look at changes how motivating it feels [11, 12].

5. When a step doesn't move

If a step's chosen time passes without Started, or a third of the time to the deadline goes by with no progress, the board asks short questions instead of nagging. At most once a day per goal; Not now is always there.

Then: "What happens if this isn't done in time?" Nothing → consider letting it go. It moves a week, fine → soft deadline. It has a price → hard deadline, earlier warnings.

6. Time, place and windows

A step can only happen when you are free, the place is open (or the time is sensible), what you need is available (car, a person), and the step before it is done. The board intersects these and shows windows; you pick one. Picking a window is a "when and where" plan, the strongest single tool in the research [1], and a specific plan also quiets the mental noise of unfinished goals [7]. Windows are offered, not forced, because rigid plans cost flexibility [7] and auto-schedulers that keep reshuffling lose people's trust [15, 16].

What the board knows (only what you tell it; no GPS, no calendar import at first):

  • My week: a simple template (work days and hours, regular evenings, Friday and Saturday habits).
  • Places: opening hours with Israeli defaults (short Friday, closed Saturday), editable: supermarket, post office, bank, offices, storage.
  • People and things: "my brother is home only Friday mornings", "the car is free at weekends".
  • The step: how long it takes and what it needs, guessed from words: call → business hours, anywhere; carry → car, daytime; buy → shop open.

What it shows: the next window and the last chance before the deadline; what missing costs ("next window: Friday next week, +7 days"); an early warning when no window is left, with three choices (change a step, move the goal, let it go); and steps that fit together by place ("you're at the supermarket Thursday; two more things can be done there"). People underestimate how long things take [8], so the board adds a buffer and learns from the times you actually mark.

7. Important vs. urgent

Urgent but unimportant things crowd out important ones, even when the important ones pay more [9]. The 2×2 matrix itself has no controlled trials and is a rule of thumb [10], so the board uses it as a lens and does the sorting for you:

  • Urgency is computed from the windows (last chance tomorrow = urgent). No manual sorting.
  • Importance is one tap (important to me / nice to have) plus your answer to "what happens if not?".
  • When you choose what to do next, the board shows why it matters; reminding people of the payoff weakened the urgency bias [9].

Important but not urgent goals get one reserved window each week and an interim deadline of their own, turning urgency into an ally, as the urgency study itself suggests [9]. We suggest keeping about three of them active; the rest wait on the shelf.

8. Wishes: not for now

Some things you want, but not now. Put them on the wish shelf. A wish has no date: it is never late, sends no reminders and does not darken your city. Each wish has a wake-up:

  • a fresh-start date (the 1st of the month, a birthday, after the holidays), when people are most ready to begin [13]; or
  • a condition ("after the storage room is sorted").

At the monthly review (the 1st) each waking wish gets one choice: make it a goal, keep it on the shelf, or let it go. A wish skipped three reviews in a row is offered for release.

New routines take weeks to become automatic, often around two months, and a single missed day does little harm [14]. Missing a day is not a reason to stop.

10. What the board will not do

  • No affirmations, no "law of attraction", no vague goals. Picturing only the dream can sap energy [2, 3].
  • No forced schedule: windows are offered, you choose [7, 15].
  • No guilt: released goals and wishes are normal outcomes.
  • No GPS tracking or calendar access unless you add it later.
  • No locked levels: you decide how much structure each goal gets. Beginners get the simplest screen first, because guidance that helps a beginner can get in an expert's way [17, 18].
